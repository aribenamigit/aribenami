*Originally published on Medium in October 2023, while Prosper was still running. Lightly edited, with the ending added in October 2026.*

In 2020 I ran a clothing brand, MEDIA IMMUNE, while I was still in school. We learned fast that digital ads rarely paid for themselves. Ad costs had been climbing for years, and most small brands spend on them anyway because there's no obvious alternative. So we turned to referrals.

Every referral tool we looked at seemed built ten years earlier. Ugly UI, worse UX. Each one lived in a pop-up, and each one asked the customer to create an account, download an app, scan a QR code, or worst of all, wait for a friend to make a purchase before getting anything.

We knew word of mouth worked better than ads. There was just no easy way for a company to integrate it or track it. So my co-founder Dylan and I spent close to two years designing, developing and building Prosper: a referral product any company could set up and scale.

## The first widget

We decided to take the whole process of sending a referral and put it into one small widget that could go anywhere on a website. The MVP shipped in October 2022.

We included more features than an MVP needed. Two-factor verification added friction we underestimated. Some screens looked too much alike. There was no way to tell how far along you were, and a progress indicator should have been there from day one.

Still, the widget integrated cleanly into client sites, we found interest from e-commerce leaders and investors, and we kept going.

## Unique codes, a brand, a website

We realized quickly that to create real value for clients and prevent fraud, every customer needed a unique discount code. We built a backend Shopify integration to generate them.

Then the brand. Prosper went through a lot of iterations before it landed on a defined style, and we built the website around it. The hard part was explaining a new kind of product to people who had never used one.

## The dashboard

The brand was built and the site was live, but the product still had to be deployed by hand, by us. So we built a dashboard where users could set up their own campaigns, design their own widgets and track their referrals.

Campaigns used variables from Prosper's backend, so anyone could decide what their referral program did. The widget creator let users design and size their widget to match their own brand. There were many requirements and many iterations. Eventually we had something good enough to launch to clients, and we did.

## The Shopify app

Then we went back to accessibility. The answer was a Shopify app. Prosper was modified to fit into Shopify's native cart and checkout pages, so it could reach customers after checkout as well as on product pages before it. Prosper was now available to millions of stores.

At that point someone could create an account, design a widget, connect it to a campaign and deploy it to their site, with Prosper hooked into their store and generating unique codes in the background.

## The second widget

By then the first widget looked dated to me, a few months after we made it. So I designed a new one. It fixed most of what users had told us: less friction, better engagement, easier referrals, and a look that set it apart. We also built an extended version with a shorter path to a referral and more context up front, and let users choose which one to run.

That's where the original post ended. "Every product has a period of fast-paced innovation followed by a period of slow optimization. I'm ready to innovate again."

## The ending

Prosper stopped operating in 2024 and was dissolved in 2025.

The product worked. For B Draddy, a golf apparel brand, a 30-day pilot sent 63 referrals, converted 38 of them, and brought in about $6,300 in net revenue at a customer acquisition cost of $1.90. Their CMO said Prosper drove more revenue in 30 days than his old tools had in three years. Across the beta, 86 businesses signed up and roughly a quarter of the discounts given out turned into a sale.

Then we went to raise. Months of due diligence with several investors, and in 2024 the only thing anyone wanted to fund was AI. The raise didn't close, and we ran out of money and energy at the same time.

That's the true story, and for a long time it was the only story I told. Here's what I'd do differently.

**Charge for the value, not the event.** Our pricing was $25 a month plus 75 cents per referral. B Draddy made $6,300 in a month and paid us about $72. We designed a product that created real value and captured almost none of it, and we called that "risk-free pricing." I'd put a real price on it from the first customer. Ten customers at $500 a month would have told us more than 86 at $25.

**Decide early what kind of company it is.** At that price point Prosper was a tool business. We raised like a venture business. Either path could have worked: a small, profitable tool that didn't need anyone's permission, or a venture company priced and positioned to grow into its round. Doing both at once is what killed it, more than the market did.

Design is verified by demand. The widget passed that test. The business model around it didn't, and I'm the one who designed both.
