Most designers I know hate this sentence: design decisions are verified by growing revenues and cash flows.

Not by awards. Not by the Dribbble post. Not by whether another designer thinks it's good. By demand.

An email that was well designed gets more clicks and more conversions than one that wasn't. A product with good packaging sells more units; in cannabis, where the flower inside is often the same, the packaging is most of the difference. A landing page that explains the product in five seconds converts better than one that takes thirty. You can measure every one of these. If the number didn't move, the design didn't work, whatever it looked like.

That cuts the other way too. You can't have good design that has no demand. A beautiful solution to a problem nobody has is a sculpture.

## The business model is design

Here's the part that took me a startup to learn. Business model design is also design, as much as hardware or UI or branding.

Pricing is a design decision. Who you sell to is a design decision. Whether you charge per seat, per month, or per outcome is a layout problem: what goes where, what's emphasized, what's hidden. It uses the same toolset: understand the user, make a choice, ship it, watch what happens, change it. And it's judged by the same test. Does demand show up, at a price that pays for the thing?

Designers who think their job ends at the interface are leaving the most important screen to someone else.

## Necessary, not sufficient

I know this because I designed both halves of a startup and only one passed. The product was verified by demand: stores installed it, customers used it, referrals converted. The pricing never was, because we never put a real price in front of a customer and watched what happened. [That story is here.](/work/prosper-product) Passing the demand test on the product and skipping it on the business model is how a product that worked ends up as a company that didn't.

## What I do now

I run marketing for a dispensary. Every piece of design I ship has a number attached, and I check it: the email's click rate, the deal sheet's redemptions, the loyalty tier's repeat rate, the discount's margin. The number is the critique. When it's bad, the design was bad, and I change it.

What I'm watching next: whether the same test holds for the things that are harder to measure. Brand. Store layout. The feeling a customer has walking in. My bet is that it does, with a longer lag. I'll know in a year.
